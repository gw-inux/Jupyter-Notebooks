The Neuman solution describes transient radial flow to a well pumping at a constant rate $Q$ in an unconfined aquifer. In contrast to the Theis solution, the Neuman solution accounts for delayed drainage from the water table and therefore captures important processes that occur during pumping in unconfined systems.

At early time, drawdown is primarily controlled by elastic storage within the aquifer. During this period, the response is similar to that of a confined aquifer and is governed by the elastic storage coefficient

$$
S_a = S_s b
$$

where $S_s$ is the specific storage and $b$ is the saturated aquifer thickness.

At later time, water-table drainage becomes increasingly important and the response is controlled by the specific yield $S_y$. The transition between these two flow regimes produces characteristic features in both drawdown and derivative plots.

The Neuman solution depends on transmissivity $T$, elastic storage $S_a$, specific yield $S_y$, and the dimensionless parameter $\beta$, which controls the timing and shape of the transition between early-time and late-time behavior.

The drawdown derivative is obtained by differentiating drawdown with respect to the natural logarithm of time:

$$
\frac{\partial s}{\partial \ln(t)}
=
t \frac{\partial s}{\partial t}
$$

The derivative emphasizes changes in drawdown slope and often reveals flow-regime transitions more clearly than the drawdown curve itself.

At early time, the derivative tends toward a Theis-like response controlled by elastic storage. At later time, delayed water-table drainage influences the derivative and produces a characteristic transition zone that is commonly observed in pumping tests conducted in unconfined aquifers.

Because transmissivity, specific yield, and the parameter $\beta$ influence different parts of the drawdown and derivative curves, derivative analysis provides valuable information for parameter estimation and conceptual model development.
